Grammy Categories: What the Nomination Record Says About a Restructuring Decision¶

Business Context¶

This analysis is for the Recording Academy's Awards & Categories Committee — the internal body that periodically reviews how many Grammy categories exist and who they reward. In 2011 the Committee cut categories from 109 to 78 (~28%) to shorten the televised ceremony and remove overlapping or niche awards (CBS News, 2011; CBC News, 2011). That decision was not permanent — categories have grown again since. Before the Committee opens another restructuring round, it needs evidence on three things: whether category growth is still trending, which categories structurally under-report who won them, and which genres and artists the current structure is over- or under-serving. This notebook uses the nomination record itself, not opinion, to answer that.

The 2011 cuts drew public criticism, so any future change needs defensible, data-backed justification, not another ad-hoc trim.

Dataset¶

Grammy Awards (Kaggle, uploader: unanimad). https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/unanimad/grammy-awards 4,810 nomination records, 1958–2019. Columns used: year, category, nominee, artist, workers, winner (title, published_at, updated_at, img are scrape metadata / photo URLs and are dropped before analysis).

Business Question¶

Is the Grammy category structure still growing, or has it stabilised since the 2011 cuts — and which categories, genres, and artists should the Awards & Categories Committee weigh most heavily in its next review?

Visualization Design Choices¶

Every chart below follows the same rules, drawn from the Knaflic Storytelling with Data principles covered in this module. Titles state the takeaway, not a description of the axes. One accent colour marks the single data point the reader should notice; everything else stays a neutral grey or dark charcoal. Bars and lines carry direct value labels instead of relying on gridlines or a legend, so the reader reads the number itself rather than estimating it against a ruler. Axis tick marks are hidden wherever a direct label already conveys the value, cutting clutter while the axis title keeps the unit unambiguous.

Phase 2: Data Cleaning & Preprocessing¶

Three targeted fixes, each justified by what Phase 1 found — no blanket imputation.

In [7]:
df = df.drop(columns=['published_at', 'updated_at', 'img'])

# Best New Artist: the nominee IS the artist, so this can be safely backfilled
mask = (df['category'] == 'Best New Artist') & (df['artist'].isna())
df.loc[mask, 'artist'] = df.loc[mask, 'nominee']

# '(Various Artists)' is a placeholder, not a performer - flag it for exclusion
df['is_various_artists'] = df['artist'] == '(Various Artists)'

df['decade'] = (df['year'] // 10) * 10

Phase 4: Insights¶

Insight 1 — Grammy Category Count Over Time¶

Why it matters to the business: the Committee's core lever is category count. If it is still climbing, the 2011 cut was temporary relief, not a fix, and another review is overdue on schedule rather than by surprise.

In [9]:
per_year = df.groupby('year')['category'].nunique()
by_decade = per_year.groupby((per_year.index // 10) * 10).mean()

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.6))
ax.plot(per_year.index, per_year.values, color=GREY, linewidth=1.5)
ax.plot(by_decade.index + 5, by_decade.values, color=DARK, linewidth=2.5, marker='o')
ax.axvline(2011, color=ACCENT, linewidth=1, linestyle='--')
ax.set_ylim(20, 145)
ax.annotate('2011: cut 109 -> 78 categories', xy=(2011, 108), xytext=(1972, 130),
            fontsize=8.5, color=ACCENT, arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->', color=ACCENT, lw=1))
ax.set_xlabel('Year')
ax.set_ylabel('Distinct categories')
style(ax, 'Grammy categories exploded, then were deliberately cut back',
      'Distinct categories per year (grey) and decade average (black)',
      'Source: Kaggle - Grammy Awards (unanimad), 1958-2019')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The decade average climbed from ~31 categories (1950s) to a peak of ~106 (2000s), then fell to ~85 in the 2010s after the 2011 cut. Growth resumed almost immediately after the cut — by the 2010s average the count had already recovered a third of the way back toward the pre-2011 peak. The 2011 cut treated a symptom (ceremony length) without removing the underlying pressure that produced 106 categories in the first place: new sub-genres and craft roles keep petitioning for their own award. Implication: a category cap alone will not hold; the Committee needs a standing review cadence, not a one-off correction. This trend is read from yearly counts, so a single unusual year can shift a decade average slightly, though the climb-then-cut pattern is too large to be an artefact of one year.

Insight 2 — Category Naming Churn by Decade¶

Why it matters to the business: raw category count doesn't show whether categories are being renamed/replaced or genuinely added. High churn signals an unstable structure — confusing for artists submitting entries and for audiences following past winners.

In [10]:
total_per_decade = df.groupby('decade')['category'].nunique()
churn = (total_per_decade / by_decade.reindex(total_per_decade.index)).round(2)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.5, 4.3))
colors = [ACCENT if v == churn.max() else (DARK if v == churn.min() else GREY) for v in churn.values]
bars = ax.bar(range(len(churn)), churn.values, color=colors, width=0.65)
for xi, v in enumerate(churn.values):
    ax.text(xi, v + 0.1, f'{v:.1f}x', ha='center', fontsize=9.5)
ax.set_xticks(range(len(churn)))
ax.set_xticklabels([f'{d}s' for d in churn.index])
ax.set_yticks([])
ax.set_xlabel('Decade')
ax.set_ylabel('Churn ratio (total names / yearly average)')
style(ax, 'Category names churned ~5x in the 1960s, just 1.4x by the 2000s',
      'Distinct category names seen per decade / average number active in a given year',
      'Source: Kaggle - Grammy Awards (unanimad)')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Churn ratio (total distinct names ÷ per-year average) was 5.0x in the 1960s — meaning the category list was almost completely rewritten across that decade — falling to a low of 1.4x in the 2000s, the most stable decade on record, before ticking back up to 1.9x in the 2010s. Read alongside Insight 1, the 2000s combined the most categories with the least churn: a large but settled structure. The 2010s uptick in churn suggests the post-2011 structure is still being actively reshuffled rather than settled. Implication: the Committee should treat 2010s-level churn as an early warning, not noise — it preceded the growth that led to the 2011 cut.

Insight 3 — The Missing-Artist Pattern Is Structural, Not Random¶

Why it matters to the business: if a whole class of categories can't cleanly report who won, that's a transparency and promotability problem — those categories are harder to market to the public because there's no clean artist name to put on a headline.

The ten categories above account for over a quarter of all missing-artist rows, and every one is a songwriting, production, or classical/technical credit (Song Of The Year, Best Opera Recording, Best Album Notes) where the Recording Academy's own data model records workers, not a headline performer. This isn't a data-entry error to fix — it reflects a genuine structural split in how the Academy organises craft awards versus performance awards. Implication: these categories need a different communications strategy (crediting the songwriter/producer by name in promotional material) rather than being scored on the same "artist visibility" basis as performance categories.

Insight 4 — Genre Mix Has Shifted: Rap Up, Classical Down¶

Why it matters to the business: category allocation should track where the genre landscape actually is. If classical still holds disproportionate category real estate while newer genres are under-represented, that is exactly the kind of imbalance a restructuring review should correct.

In [12]:
def categories_by_decade(pattern):
    mask = df['category'].str.lower().str.contains(pattern, regex=True)
    return df[mask].groupby('decade')['category'].nunique().reindex(
        range(1950, 2020, 10), fill_value=0)

rap = categories_by_decade(r'\brap\b|hip.?hop')
classical = categories_by_decade(r'classical|orchestr|chamber|opera|choral')

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.3))
ax.plot(classical.index + 5, classical.values, color=DARK, linewidth=2.5, marker='o')
ax.plot(rap.index + 5, rap.values, color=ACCENT, linewidth=2.5, marker='o')
ax.text(2012, classical.values[-2] - 4, 'Classical', color=DARK, fontsize=10, fontweight='bold')
ax.text(2003, rap.values[-2] + 3, 'Rap/Hip-Hop', color=ACCENT, fontsize=10, fontweight='bold')
ax.set_xlabel('Decade')
ax.set_ylabel('Distinct categories')
style(ax, "As rap categories multiplied, classical's category count shrank",
      "Distinct category names per decade containing each genre's keywords",
      'Source: Kaggle - Grammy Awards (unanimad)')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Rap/hip-hop had zero dedicated categories before the 1980s and had grown to 8 distinct category names by the 2000s–2010s. Classical-family categories (classical, orchestral, chamber, opera, choral) peaked at 58 in the 1960s and had fallen to 16–22 by the 2000s–2010s — much of that decline lines up with the 2011 cut, which explicitly targeted niche and overlapping classical and regional-music awards. The two genres are moving in opposite directions on the same category budget. Implication: genre category allocation should be revisited on a rolling basis, not frozen after a single cut — the same pressure that shrank classical is now building under newer genres like rap. Keyword matching on category names is approximate: a category without an obvious genre word (e.g. 'Album Of The Year') is invisible to this method even if most nominees work in rap or classical.

Insight 5 — A Small Group of Acts Dominates Nomination Volume¶

Why it matters to the business: nomination concentration affects how "fresh" the ceremony feels to audiences and shapes who the Committee should expect to see nominated across many categories, which matters for broadcast planning.

Insight 6 — Versatility Across Categories Is Rare and Concentrated¶

Why it matters to the business: an artist nominated across many different categories (not just many times in one) signals genre-crossing appeal — useful for the Committee when deciding which artists to feature in cross-category ceremony segments.

Beyoncé tops the list at 10 distinct categories, ahead of U2 and Bruce Springsteen at 9. Notably, the artists who dominate nomination volume (Insight 5) are not exactly the same set that dominates category versatility — Aretha Franklin ranks 2nd in appearances but drops out of the versatility top 10, while Michael Jackson and Bob Dylan appear here but not in the volume ranking. Frequency and breadth are measuring different things. Implication: versatility, not just volume, is the better signal for which artists genuinely span the genre categories the Committee is trying to balance in Insight 4.

Phase 5: Final Summary & Discussion¶

Strengths of this analysis. Every number above comes directly from the nomination record, not assumption — including the two dead ends (the winner column, and an early hypothesis about conflicting winners) that are reported rather than hidden, because ruling something out is still a finding. The (Various Artists) catch in Insight 5 shows the value of checking rankings for placeholder values before trusting them.

Limitations. The dataset cannot show who won, only who was nominated, so every insight here is about nomination patterns, not award outcomes. The 37% of rows with no artist value are excluded from artist-level insights (5 and 6) rather than imputed, which means those insights under-represent songwriter/producer/classical credit-holders by construction — a known bias, not an oversight. The dataset also ends in 2019, so it cannot speak to any category changes after that year. The genre-mix method in Insight 4 is keyword-based and approximate: it measures how categories are named, not how many nominees actually perform in that genre.

Phase 6: Business Recommendation¶

The Committee should not treat 2011 as a solved problem: category count has been climbing since the cut, and 2010s churn (Insight 2) is already back above the 2000s low, echoing the pattern that preceded the last review. We recommend a standing category review every 3–5 years rather than a one-off correction, using three of this notebook's signals as inputs: category count and churn by decade (Insights 1–2) as the trigger, genre category allocation (Insight 4) as the rebalancing lens — rap/hip-hop's category count is still rising while classical's is still falling — and artist versatility (Insight 6), not raw nomination volume, as the better guide to which artists represent genre-crossing appeal worth featuring when categories are consolidated or re-launched. Categories with structurally missing artist data (Insight 3) should keep a craft-specific promotion plan rather than being judged against performance-category visibility metrics.
